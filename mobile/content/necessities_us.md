# Necessities: What You NEED to Settle In (US)

These are not legal requirements, but you will want all of them sorted in your first week or two. They are the practical layer that makes daily life work.

---

## 1. A US Phone Plan / SIM Card

**What it is:**
A US phone number and mobile data plan on a local carrier.

**Why you need it:**
Your home-country SIM will either not work or will charge very high roaming rates. In the US, almost everything — two-factor authentication, delivery notifications, Uber, healthcare portals, university communication — assumes you have a reachable US number. Many apartment applications and job forms ask for one too.

**What to look for:**
Prepaid (no-contract) plans from carriers like T-Mobile, AT&T, Verizon, or budget MVNOs (Mint Mobile, Visible, Cricket) are easy to set up without a US credit history. University towns often have carrier stores within walking distance of campus. Bring your unlocked phone if you can — it saves buying a new device.

*Compare plan coverage in your specific city using each carrier's own coverage map tool before you buy.*

---

## 2. A Transit Card or Fare Account

**What it is:**
Most US cities with public transit use a reloadable card or a linked account for buses and trains. Examples: CharlieCard (Boston), Ventra (Chicago), OMNY/MetroCard (New York City), Clipper (Bay Area), TAP (Los Angeles), ORCA (Seattle).

**Why you need it:**
Paying with cash on transit is often not accepted on rail or requires exact change on buses. The card is also usually cheaper per ride and lets you set up auto-reload so you are never stranded.

**What to look for:**
Many transit systems offer a reduced student or youth fare — check if your university has a partnership with the local transit authority (some schools bundle a transit pass into your student ID or offer deeply discounted semester passes).

*Verify the transit card system in your city at that city's transit authority website (e.g., mbta.com for Boston, mta.info for NYC). Student discount programs vary by school — check your university's transportation or commuter services page.*

## 3. Basic Furniture and Household Essentials

**What it is:**
The minimum you need to sleep, eat, and study: a bed frame and mattress (or at least a mattress pad), bedding, a pillow, towels, basic cookware and cutlery if you have a kitchen, and a desk lamp.

**Why you need it:**
University housing is often furnished only at a basic level, and off-campus apartments are usually empty. Buying everything new is expensive, and you likely cannot bring much from home.

**What to look for:**
- **Facebook Marketplace and Craigslist** — students moving out at the end of a semester often list furniture for free or very cheap
- **University "free and for sale" boards or groups** — many campuses have these specifically for students
- **Goodwill and thrift stores** — common near university towns, good for kitchen basics and bedding
- **IKEA or Target** — for new items, especially if the used market in your area is thin

Plan your first night: even if everything else takes time, have bedding and a towel before your first night in your room.

*No official source for this — practical community knowledge. Your university's student affairs or housing office may have a "what to bring" checklist worth reading before you arrive.*

## 4. Groceries and Basic Food Setup

**What it is:**
Finding your nearest grocery store and setting up a basic pantry so you can feed yourself without relying on takeout for every meal.

**Why you need it:**
Eating out every meal in the US is expensive and adds up quickly. Even a modest ability to cook simple meals saves significant money over a semester.

**What to look for:**
- **Large chains** (Walmart, Target, Kroger, Safeway, Stop & Shop, HEB, Publix — varies by region) tend to have the widest selection and lowest prices
- **Ethnic grocery stores** — many university towns have South Asian, East Asian, Latin, or Middle Eastern grocery stores that carry ingredients you may be familiar with and sometimes at lower prices than mainstream chains
- **Aldi and Lidl** — discount chains with solid basics at very low prices, found in many cities
- **Student meal plans** — if your university has a dining hall and you are required or opt to have a meal plan, understand how it works and what the balance rollover policy is

Stock your first week with staples: rice or pasta, cooking oil, eggs, bread, a few vegetables, and whatever your home-country pantry basics are if you can find them.

*Your university's international student orientation materials often include a local grocery guide — worth checking before or just after you arrive.*

## 5. Weather-Appropriate Clothing

**What it is:**
Clothing suited to the climate of the specific US city you are living in, which can vary dramatically from what you are used to.

**Why you need it:**
The US is geographically huge and has extremely varied climates. A student arriving in Minnesota in August needs a very different wardrobe than one arriving in Miami. The US also tends toward aggressive indoor air conditioning in summer — buildings can feel cold even on hot days.

**What to look for:**
- **If your city has cold winters** (most of the Northeast, Midwest, and parts of the Pacific Northwest): you will need a heavy coat, boots with grip for ice and snow, thermal underlayers, gloves, and a hat. These are hard to find cheaply mid-winter — buy before the season hits.
- **If your city is hot and humid** (Southeast, Texas, Florida): lightweight and breathable fabrics are a priority. Bring layers for the indoors air conditioning.
- **If your city has both** (Chicago, New York, Boston): you will need a range across the year.

Thrift stores are a good source for coats and cold-weather gear. Your campus may also have a clothing exchange or free store.

*Check your city's historical average temperature range — the US National Weather Service (weather.gov) has this data by location.*
