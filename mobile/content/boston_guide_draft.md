## Banking

Boston has a strong presence of major US banks. When you first arrive, you will likely need to open a checking account to receive payments, pay rent, and avoid foreign transaction fees on everyday spending.

Most large banks — such as Bank of America, Chase, Citizens Bank, and TD Bank — have branches near major universities and in the city centre. Many of these will open a student checking account with your passport, your Form I-20, and a US address. Some will also ask for a minimum opening deposit.

If you do not yet have a Social Security Number (SSN), some fintech services can issue you a US account number and routing number without one. These can be useful in the weeks immediately after arrival before your SSN application is processed.

Look out for student-specific accounts that waive monthly maintenance fees, and check whether your home country's bank has a US partnership that could reduce wire transfer costs when money is sent from family abroad.

> Verify current requirements: bank account requirements and fee structures change. Visit the branch or check the bank's website for the most up-to-date terms before opening an account.

## Healthcare

Healthcare in the United States is expensive without insurance, and Boston is no exception. Most universities in the Boston area — including Boston University, Northeastern, MIT, and Harvard — require all enrolled students to have health insurance and will automatically enrol you in their Student Health Plan unless you actively waive it.

Before waiving the university plan, make sure any alternative plan you have meets your school's minimum coverage requirements. The university health plan is often the most straightforward option because it is tailored to students and accepted at the campus health centre.

Boston has several world-class hospitals — including Massachusetts General Hospital, Brigham and Women's Hospital, and Boston Medical Center — but visiting these without insurance can be extremely costly. Your campus health centre should be your first stop for routine care and referrals.

Some universities offer mental health counselling and wellness services as part of the student health fee. Find out what your school includes before looking for external providers.

> Verify current details: health insurance plan offerings and waiver deadlines change each academic year. Check your university's student health services website for current requirements, costs, and deadlines.

## SIM / Phone

Getting a local US phone number as soon as you arrive in Boston will make many administrative tasks much easier — bank accounts, university registration, and landlords often expect a US number.

The major national carriers with good coverage in Boston are T-Mobile, AT&T, and Verizon. Prepaid SIM cards from these carriers (or their sub-brands like Metro by T-Mobile or Cricket Wireless) are available at carrier stores, major supermarkets, and convenience stores without requiring a credit check or Social Security Number.

Prepaid plans typically offer a monthly data allowance and unlimited domestic calls and texts. When you are more settled, you can switch to a postpaid plan, which is often cheaper per month but requires a credit check or a deposit if you have no US credit history.

International SIM providers and eSIM services can also bridge the gap during your first few days if you are arriving without a local number.

> Verify current details: carrier pricing, coverage maps, and plan structures change regularly. Check the carrier's website or visit a store to confirm current prepaid options available in the Boston area.

## Transport

Boston's public transport network is operated by the MBTA (Massachusetts Bay Transportation Authority), commonly known as the T. It covers the city and many surrounding suburbs.

The subway (the T) has several colour-coded lines and connects most major university campuses to the city centre. The commuter rail extends to further-out areas. Buses fill in coverage gaps, particularly in neighborhoods not served by the subway.

The CharlieCard is a reusable transit card that gives you a reduced fare compared to paying cash each time. You can load money onto it at fare machines or with the MBTA app.

Cycling is also popular, and Boston has a bike-share scheme (Bluebikes) with docking stations near most campuses. Many students find a combination of the T and a bicycle covers most of their daily travel needs.

Uber and Lyft operate widely in Boston and can be useful for late nights or trips not well served by public transit.

> Verify current details: MBTA fares, service routes, and schedules change. Check the MBTA website (mbta.com) for current fares, maps, and any planned service changes before you rely on a specific route.

## Cost of Living

Boston is one of the more expensive cities in the United States, particularly for housing and food. Being in a university city, there is a wide range of options at different price points, but it is important to budget carefully.

**Housing** is typically the largest expense. On-campus housing is often more convenient for new arrivals and includes utilities, but spots can be limited and must be applied for early. Off-campus shared apartments in neighborhoods close to major universities — such as Allston, Brighton, Cambridge, or Somerville — are popular with students and tend to be more affordable per person when sharing.

**Food** costs can vary widely. Cooking at home using grocery stores is significantly cheaper than eating out regularly. Boston has a strong culture of local markets and ethnic grocery stores where ingredients can be found at reasonable prices. Campus dining halls may or may not be cost-effective depending on your meal plan.

**Transport** is more affordable than in many comparable cities if you rely on the T rather than taxis or rideshare apps. Monthly and semester passes are available and can reduce per-trip costs significantly.

**Entertainment and social life** in Boston is enriched by the fact that many museums, lectures, and cultural events offer free or heavily discounted admission to students.

> Verify current details: costs change with inflation and the housing market. Do not treat any figures you find online as reliable; check current rental listings, your university's cost of living estimates, and consult current students for a realistic picture before finalising your budget.
