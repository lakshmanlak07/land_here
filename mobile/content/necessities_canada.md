# Necessities: What You NEED to Settle In (Canada)

These are not legal requirements, but you will want them sorted in your first week or two. They are the practical layer that makes daily life in Canada work.

---

## 1. A Canadian SIM Card / Phone Plan

**What it is:**
A Canadian mobile number and data plan on a local carrier.

**Why you need it:**
Your home SIM will roam expensively. In Canada, bank setup, university portals, two-factor logins, delivery texts, and most apps assume a reachable Canadian number. Canada also has comparatively high mobile prices, so it pays to shop around.

**What to look for:**
Prepaid or month-to-month plans from the big carriers (Rogers, Bell, Telus) or their cheaper sub-brands (Fido, Koodo, Virgin Plus) and budget players (Public Mobile, Freedom Mobile, Lucky Mobile) are available without a credit check or SIN. Bring an unlocked phone. Coverage in rural areas can vary a lot between carriers, so check for your specific city.

*Verify coverage for your city on each carrier's own coverage map before buying. The CRTC (crtc.gc.ca) publishes your rights as a wireless customer.*

---

## 2. A Transit Card or Fare Account

**What it is:**
The local reloadable transit card or app for buses, streetcars, light rail, and metro. Examples: **Presto** (Toronto/GTA and much of Ontario), **OPUS** (Montreal), **Compass** (Metro Vancouver), and city systems in Calgary, Edmonton, Ottawa, and others.

**Why you need it:**
Transit cards are cheaper per ride than cash, let you transfer between services, and support auto-reload. Cash fares often require exact change and don't include free transfers in the same way.

**What to look for:**
Many cities and universities offer a discounted student transit pass — sometimes a **U-Pass** bundled into your student fees (common in Vancouver, Ottawa, and elsewhere). Check whether your tuition already includes a transit pass before buying one separately.

*Verify your city's fare system on that transit authority's official site (e.g. the regional transit agency for your city). U-Pass and student-discount details are on your university's transportation page.*

## 3. Winter-Ready Clothing (Do Not Underestimate This)

**What it is:**
Genuine cold-weather clothing suited to Canadian winters, which in most of the country are long, snowy, and far colder than many newcomers expect.

**Why you need it:**
Outside the mild south coast of British Columbia, Canadian winters are seriously cold — frequently well below freezing, with wind chill making it feel colder still. Inadequate clothing is not just uncomfortable, it is unsafe. This is the single most important practical purchase for most students.

**What to look for:**
- A proper insulated **winter parka** rated for cold temperatures (look for a temperature rating, not just a fashion coat)
- **Waterproof, insulated boots** with grip for ice and snow
- **Toque (warm hat), insulated gloves/mittens, and a scarf or neck gaiter** — you lose a lot of heat from head and hands
- **Thermal base layers** to layer under everyday clothes

Buy before or right at the start of winter; good winter gear sells out and is hard to find cheaply mid-season. End-of-season sales (late winter) are great for next year. Thrift stores (Value Village, etc.) and campus clothing swaps can help.

*Check your specific city's winter climate before packing — Environment and Climate Change Canada (weather.gc.ca) publishes regional averages and forecasts.*

## 4. Basic Furniture and Household Essentials

**What it is:**
The minimum to sleep, eat, and study: a bed and mattress (or mattress pad), bedding for a Canadian winter, towels, basic cookware and cutlery, and a desk lamp.

**Why you need it:**
University residences are often partly furnished, but off-campus apartments are usually empty. Warm bedding matters more here than in milder climates.

**What to look for:**
- **Facebook Marketplace and Kijiji** — students moving out list furniture cheap or free, especially around term changes
- **University buy/sell groups** — common on most campuses
- **Thrift stores** (Value Village, Salvation Army) — good for kitchenware and bedding
- **IKEA, Walmart, Canadian Tire, Dollarama** — affordable new basics

Plan your first night: have warm bedding and a towel ready before you arrive.

*No official source — practical community knowledge. Your university's housing or international office may publish a "what to bring" checklist worth reading first.*

## 5. Groceries and Basic Food Setup

**What it is:**
Finding your nearest grocery store and stocking a basic pantry so you are not buying every meal.

**Why you need it:**
Eating out for every meal is expensive in Canada. Simple home cooking saves significantly over a term, and groceries can be cheaper at the right stores.

**What to look for:**
- **Discount chains** (No Frills, FreshCo, Food Basics, Walmart, Real Canadian Superstore) — lowest prices on staples
- **Mainstream chains** (Loblaws, Metro, Sobeys, Save-On-Foods) — wider selection; loyalty programs (PC Optimum) lower prices
- **Ethnic and international grocers** — most Canadian cities have South Asian, East Asian, Caribbean, and Middle Eastern stores with familiar ingredients, often cheaper for spices, rice, and lentils
- **Student meal plans** — if your residence includes one, understand what it covers and the rollover policy before buying extra

Stock first-week staples: rice or pasta, oil, eggs, bread, some vegetables, and your own pantry basics if you can find them.

*Your university's international orientation materials often include a local grocery guide — check it on or before arrival.*
