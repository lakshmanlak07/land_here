# Necessities: What You NEED to Settle In (Australia)

These are not legal requirements, but you will want all of them sorted in your first week or two. They are the practical layer that makes daily life work.

---

## 1. An Australian Phone Plan / SIM Card

**What it is:**
An Australian mobile number and data plan on a local carrier.

**Why you need it:**
Your home-country SIM will roam at high rates or not work well. In Australia almost everything — two-factor authentication, MyGov and bank logins, rideshare, delivery, and university communication — assumes a reachable Australian number. Many rental applications and casual job forms ask for one too.

**What to look for:**
Prepaid (no-lock-in) SIMs are easy to set up without local credit history. The big networks are Telstra (widest regional coverage), Optus, and Vodafone; cheaper resellers (Amaysim, Boost — which uses the Telstra network, Belong, Aldi Mobile) run on the same towers for less. You can often buy and activate a SIM at the airport, a supermarket, or a phone shop near campus. Bring an unlocked phone if you can.

*Compare coverage for your specific city or regional campus using each carrier's own coverage map before you buy.*

## 2. A Public Transport Smartcard

**What it is:**
Most Australian states use a reloadable transport smartcard or contactless tap-on system for trains, buses, trams, and ferries. Examples: Opal (New South Wales / Sydney), Myki (Victoria / Melbourne), Go card (Queensland / Brisbane), Metrocard (Adelaide), SmartRider (Perth).

**Why you need it:**
Paper tickets are often more expensive or not accepted, and these systems cap your daily and weekly spend so you are not overcharged. Auto top-up means you are never stranded at a gate.

**What to look for:**
Many states offer a concession (student) fare, but eligibility for international students varies a lot by state — in some states full-fee international students are **not** eligible for concession travel. Check before assuming you qualify. Some cities now also accept contactless bank-card tap-on.

*Verify the card and any student-concession eligibility on your state transport authority's website (e.g. transportnsw.info for Opal, ptv.vic.gov.au for Myki). Rules differ by state.*

## 3. Basic Furniture and Household Essentials

**What it is:**
The minimum to sleep, eat, and study: a bed and mattress, bedding, a pillow, towels, basic kitchenware, and a desk lamp.

**Why you need it:**
Student accommodation ranges from fully furnished to completely empty, and most private "share house" rooms come unfurnished. Buying everything new is expensive.

**What to look for:**
- **Facebook Marketplace and Gumtree** — the main second-hand markets; students moving out at semester's end list furniture cheap or free.
- **University accommodation and student Facebook groups** — often have buy/sell/giveaway posts.
- **Op shops (charity stores)** like Vinnies, Salvos, and Savers — good for kitchenware and bedding.
- **IKEA, Kmart, Target, and Big W** — affordable new basics.

Plan your first night: have bedding and a towel sorted before you arrive or on day one.

*No official source — practical community knowledge. Your university's accommodation or student services page may have an arrival checklist worth reading first.*

## 4. Groceries and Basic Food Setup

**What it is:**
Finding your nearest supermarket and setting up a basic pantry so you are not buying every meal.

**Why you need it:**
Eating out in Australia is expensive and adds up fast. Cooking even simple meals saves a lot over a semester.

**What to look for:**
- **Coles and Woolworths** — the two big national chains, everywhere.
- **Aldi** — noticeably cheaper for staples; widely available in cities.
- **Local markets and ethnic grocers** — most university cities have South Asian, East Asian, and Middle Eastern grocers with familiar ingredients, often cheaper for spices, rice, and lentils.
- **Specials and "half price" cycles** — the big chains rotate weekly specials; their apps show them.

Stock your first week with staples: rice or pasta, oil, eggs, bread, a few vegetables, and your home-country basics if you can find them.

*Your university's international student orientation often includes a local food/grocery guide — check it before or just after you arrive.*

## 5. Weather-Appropriate Clothing

**What it is:**
Clothing suited to your specific Australian city, and a mindset adjustment about the seasons.

**Why you need it:**
Australia's seasons are **reversed** from the Northern Hemisphere — summer is roughly December–February, winter June–August. Climates vary enormously: Darwin and Cairns are tropical, Melbourne is famously "four seasons in one day," and Canberra and the alpine regions get genuinely cold. The sun is also very strong almost everywhere.

**What to look for:**
- **Sun protection year-round** — a hat, sunglasses, and high-SPF sunscreen; UV levels are high even on cool days.
- **For southern cities (Melbourne, Canberra, Hobart, Adelaide):** layers and a warm jacket for winter; it gets cold and wet.
- **For northern/tropical cities (Brisbane, Cairns, Darwin):** lightweight, breathable clothing and rain gear for the wet season.
- Op shops are a good cheap source for jackets and winter layers.

*Check your city's seasonal averages on the Bureau of Meteorology (bom.gov.au) before you pack or shop.*
