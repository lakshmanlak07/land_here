# Necessities: What You NEED to Settle In (Germany)

These are not legal requirements, but you will want all of them sorted in your first week or two. They are the practical layer that makes daily life work.

---

## 1. A German Phone Plan / SIM Card

**What it is:**
A German mobile number and data plan on a local network.

**Why you need it:**
Your home SIM will roam expensively, and German services — bank logins, two-factor codes, appointment bookings (Termine), and university portals — assume a reachable German number. Some processes still send codes by SMS.

**What to look for:**
Prepaid (Prepaid-SIM) plans are easiest without local credit history. The main networks are Telekom (best coverage), Vodafone, and O2/Telefónica; cheaper resellers (Aldi Talk, congstar, Lidl Connect, Lebara) run on the same networks for less. Note: German law requires SIM registration with ID, so you'll verify your identity (passport) when activating. You can buy SIMs in supermarkets, electronics shops (e.g. Saturn, MediaMarkt), and phone shops.

*Compare coverage for your specific city using each network's coverage map before you buy.*

---

## 2. A Public Transport Ticket (incl. the Deutschlandticket)

**What it is:**
Germany has excellent public transport (U-Bahn, S-Bahn, trams, buses). Most students travel on a monthly pass rather than single tickets. The nationwide **Deutschlandticket** is a flat monthly subscription valid on regional and local public transport across the whole country.

**Why you need it:**
Single tickets are expensive and you can be fined heavily for riding without a valid ticket (controls are frequent and plain-clothed). A monthly pass or the Deutschlandticket is far cheaper if you travel regularly.

**What to look for:**
Many universities include a **Semesterticket** in your enrolment fees that covers local transport — check whether yours does before buying anything separately. Some unis offer a discounted student version of the Deutschlandticket. Validate/activate tickets correctly (some require activation before boarding).

*Verify what's included in your university's Semesterticket via your student union (AStA/Studierendenwerk), and Deutschlandticket details on the official transit/operator site for your region.*

## 3. Basic Furniture and Household Essentials

**What it is:**
The minimum to sleep, eat, and study: a bed and mattress, bedding, a pillow, towels, basic kitchenware, and a lamp.

**Why you need it:**
German rentals are often **completely unfurnished** — sometimes without even a fitted kitchen or light fixtures. Student dorm rooms (Wohnheim) are usually furnished, but private flats and WG (shared flat) rooms vary widely.

**What to look for:**
- **eBay Kleinanzeigen (now Kleinanzeigen)** — the dominant second-hand marketplace; lots of cheap or free furniture, especially "zu verschenken" (to give away).
- **WG and student Facebook/Telegram groups** — buy/sell and giveaway posts.
- **Sozialkaufhaus / second-hand stores** — charity furniture shops in many cities.
- **IKEA and discounters (e.g. Höffner, XXXLutz)** — affordable new basics.

Plan your first night: have bedding and a towel ready before you arrive.

*No official source — practical community knowledge. Your university's Studierendenwerk (student services) often has arrival and accommodation guidance worth reading first.*

## 4. Groceries and Basic Food Setup

**What it is:**
Finding your nearest supermarket and setting up a basic pantry.

**Why you need it:**
Cooking at home is much cheaper than eating out, and German supermarket prices are among the lowest in Western Europe if you shop at discounters.

**What to look for:**
- **Discounters: Aldi and Lidl** — very cheap, good quality basics; **Netto** and **Penny** too.
- **Full-range supermarkets: Rewe and Edeka** — wider selection, slightly pricier.
- **Asian / Turkish / international grocers** — most cities have well-stocked shops with familiar ingredients, often cheaper for rice, spices, and lentils.
- **Bring coins/own bags:** you pay a small **Pfand** (deposit) on many bottles and cans — return them at machines (Pfandautomat) to get it back. Bags cost extra; bring your own.

Note that most supermarkets are **closed on Sundays** — shop ahead. Stock staples: bread, pasta/rice, oil, eggs, vegetables.

*Your university's international office or Studierendenwerk often shares a local food/cost-of-living guide — check it on arrival.*

## 5. Weather-Appropriate Clothing

**What it is:**
Clothing for a temperate four-season climate that is colder and greyer than many newcomers expect.

**Why you need it:**
German winters are cold, dark, and often wet, roughly November–March, with short daylight hours. Summers are mild to warm. Rain is possible in any season.

**What to look for:**
- **A genuinely warm, waterproof winter coat**, plus a hat, gloves, and a scarf — buy before winter sets in.
- **Waterproof shoes/boots** and an umbrella or rain jacket.
- **Layers** for the shoulder seasons (autumn/spring) which swing a lot.
- Second-hand shops (Secondhand-Läden) and flea markets (Flohmarkt) are good cheap sources for winter coats.

*Check your city's seasonal averages on the German Weather Service (dwd.de) before you pack or shop.*
