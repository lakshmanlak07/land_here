# Mandatories: What You MUST Do After Arriving in the US

These are not optional. They are legal or institutional requirements for international students on an F-1 or J-1 visa. Missing them can put your visa status at risk. Work through them in roughly this order.

---

## 1. SEVIS Check-In (Report Your Arrival)

**What it is:**
SEVIS (Student and Exchange Visitor Information System) is the US government database that tracks international students. When you arrive in the US, your school's international office must mark you as "active" in SEVIS. This is called check-in or arrival registration.

**Why it matters:**
If you are not checked in within a required window after your program start date, you can fall out of legal student status — even if you did nothing wrong otherwise. Your entire visa depends on being active in SEVIS.

**What you do:**
Most schools ask you to fill out an online arrival form or visit the international student office in person before or on a set deadline. Your I-20 (F-1) or DS-2019 (J-1) document lists your program start date — use that to understand your timeline.

*Verify the exact deadline and process on the official source: studyinthestates.dhs.gov — [SEVIS section for students]*

---

## 2. Report to Your International Student Office

**What it is:**
Your university's international student office (often called the ISO, ISSO, ISS, or OIA) is the office that manages your I-20 or DS-2019 and maintains your SEVIS record.

**Why it matters:**
They are the people who keep your status valid. You must register with them at the start of every semester and whenever anything in your life changes — your address, your program, your funding. They are also your first call if anything goes wrong.

**What you do:**
Find your school's international office website, complete any required arrival check-in form, and attend any mandatory orientation they run for new international students.

*Verify requirements at your specific institution's international student office website. General framework: studyinthestates.dhs.gov*

---

## 3. Register for Classes (Full-Time Enrollment)

**What it is:**
As an F-1 student, you are required by law to maintain full-time enrollment every semester (except your final semester, under certain conditions). "Full-time" is defined by your school, but is typically 12 credit hours for undergraduates and 9 for graduate students.

**Why it matters:**
Dropping below full-time without prior authorization from your international office is a violation of your F-1 status. It can trigger SEVIS termination and affect your ability to stay in the US.

**What you do:**
Register for the required number of credits through your school's registration system before each semester begins. If you ever need to drop below full-time for medical or academic reasons, get written approval from your international office first — do not just drop a class.

*Verify the full-time requirement for your specific visa type: studyinthestates.dhs.gov — [Maintaining Status section]*

---

## 4. Enroll in Health Insurance

**What it is:**
Most US universities require all students — especially international students — to have health insurance while enrolled. Many schools offer their own Student Health Insurance Plan (SHIP). In the US, healthcare is expensive without insurance; a single hospital visit can cost thousands of dollars out of pocket.

**Why it matters:**
It is often a condition of enrollment. Beyond that, it is your financial protection. International students are generally not eligible for US government health programs like Medicaid, so the university plan (or a comparable private plan) is your main option.

**What you do:**
Check your school's bursar or student health portal. Many schools auto-enroll you and charge the premium to your student account. If you have comparable coverage elsewhere, there is often a waiver process — but you must apply by a deadline.

*Verify your school's specific plan, waiver options, and deadlines through your university's student health or bursar office. General guidance: healthcare.gov (for understanding US insurance concepts)*

---

## 5. Open a US Bank Account

**What it is:**
A US bank account lets you receive money (scholarships, stipends, wire transfers), pay rent, and manage everyday expenses without paying foreign transaction fees on every purchase.

**Why it matters:**
Without a US account, you will rely entirely on international cards, which typically carry fees on every transaction and may not be accepted everywhere. Some landlords and employers can only pay via US bank transfer or check.

**What you do:**
Visit a bank branch in person — most major banks (Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, etc.) and many credit unions have branches near university campuses. Bring your passport, visa, I-20 or DS-2019, proof of your US address (a lease, a letter from your school), and your school ID. Some banks designed for students or immigrants have lighter documentation requirements.

*Verify what documents each bank requires before you visit. No single government source covers this — check directly with the bank. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (consumerfinance.gov) has plain-English guides to understanding US bank accounts.*

---

## 6. Apply for a Social Security Number (If Eligible)

**What it is:**
A Social Security Number (SSN) is a 9-digit US government ID number used for employment, taxes, and many financial services. Not every international student is eligible — you generally need to have authorized employment (like an on-campus job or a funded assistantship) before you can apply.

**Why it matters:**
If you have any form of paid work in the US, you need an SSN to be paid legally and to file taxes. It is also used as an ID for things like credit applications and some lease agreements. If you are not working, you may not be eligible and do not need to apply right away.

**What you do:**
If you have an offer of on-campus employment or authorized work, ask your international office for a letter confirming your authorization and employment. Then visit your local Social Security Administration (SSA) office in person with your passport, visa, I-20/DS-2019, and the SSA letter. You should wait at least 10 days after entering the US before applying so that your entry records have time to appear in the SSA's system.

*Verify current eligibility rules and required documents at: ssa.gov — [Social Security Numbers for Noncitizens section]*
