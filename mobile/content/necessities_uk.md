# Necessities: What You NEED to Settle In (UK)

These are not legal requirements, but you will want them sorted in your first week or two. They are the practical layer that makes daily life in the UK work.

---

## 1. A UK SIM Card / Phone Plan

**What it is:**
A UK mobile number and data plan on a local network.

**Why you need it:**
Your home-country SIM will roam expensively or not work well. In the UK, bank verification, university systems, two-factor logins, delivery texts, and NHS bookings all assume a reachable UK number. Many forms ask for one.

**What to look for:**
Pay-as-you-go (PAYG) SIMs from EE, O2, Vodafone, or Three, or cheaper sub-brands (giffgaff, Smarty, Lebara, VOXI), are sold in supermarkets and phone shops and need no contract or credit history. Bring an unlocked phone if you can. SIM-only monthly rolling plans are good value once you have a UK bank account.

*Verify coverage for your specific city using each network's own coverage map before you buy. Ofcom (ofcom.org.uk) publishes guidance on choosing a mobile provider.*

---

## 2. A Transit Card or Contactless Travel Setup

**What it is:**
The local way to pay for buses, trams, and trains. In London this is the **Oyster card** or simply tapping a contactless bank card / phone; other cities have their own systems (e.g. travel passes in Manchester, Birmingham, Glasgow, Edinburgh).

**Why you need it:**
Contactless and smartcards are cheaper than paper tickets and often have daily/weekly fare caps that save money automatically. Cash is increasingly not accepted on many buses.

**What to look for:**
In London, contactless and Oyster both apply automatic fare caps — and 16–25 / student railcards can be linked for discounts. Outside London, check the local transport operator for student passes; your university may bundle or discount travel. National rail journeys are much cheaper with a **16–25 Railcard** (or the **26–30 Railcard**) if you are eligible.

*Verify the system for your city on that operator's official site (e.g. tfl.gov.uk for London). Railcard eligibility and savings are at railcard.co.uk. Student discounts vary — check your university's site.*

## 3. Basic Furniture and Household Essentials

**What it is:**
The minimum to sleep, eat, and study: bedding and a duvet, a pillow, towels, basic kitchenware if your accommodation has a kitchen, and a desk lamp.

**Why you need it:**
University halls are often part-furnished (bed, desk, wardrobe) but rarely include bedding or kitchen items. Private rentals vary from furnished to completely empty.

**What to look for:**
- **Facebook Marketplace and Gumtree** — students leaving at term's end often sell or give away furniture cheaply
- **University buy/sell groups** — most campuses have these
- **Charity shops** (British Heart Foundation, Oxfam, etc.) — good for kitchenware, bedding, and small furniture
- **Argos, IKEA, Wilko-style value shops, and supermarkets** — for affordable new basics

Plan your first night: have a duvet, pillow, and towel ready before you arrive.

*No official source — this is practical community knowledge. Your university's accommodation office often publishes a "what to bring" list worth reading first.*

## 4. Groceries and Basic Food Setup

**What it is:**
Locating your nearest supermarket and stocking a basic pantry so you are not buying every meal.

**Why you need it:**
Eating out or ordering in for every meal adds up fast. Even simple home cooking saves a lot over a term.

**What to look for:**
- **Budget supermarkets** (Aldi, Lidl) — lowest prices on staples
- **Mainstream chains** (Tesco, Sainsbury's, Asda, Morrisons) — wide selection, frequent meal-deal offers, and loyalty cards (Clubcard, Nectar) that lower prices
- **Local and ethnic grocers** — many UK towns have South Asian, African-Caribbean, East Asian, and Middle Eastern shops with familiar ingredients, often cheaper for spices, rice, and lentils
- **Student / hall catering** — if your accommodation is catered, understand what is included before buying extra

Stock first-week staples: rice or pasta, oil, eggs, bread, a few vegetables, tea, and your own pantry basics if you can find them.

*Your university's international orientation pack often lists local shops and markets — check it on arrival.*

## 5. Weather-Appropriate Clothing (and a Good Waterproof)

**What it is:**
Clothing for the UK climate: mild but very changeable, often grey, damp, and windy, with cool-to-cold winters and limited extreme heat.

**Why you need it:**
The UK is rarely freezing for long, but it is frequently wet and windy, and many buildings can feel cold and damp. The single most useful item is a genuinely **waterproof jacket** plus warm layers — far more than a single heavy coat.

**What to look for:**
- A waterproof, windproof jacket with a hood (an umbrella alone loses to UK wind)
- Layering pieces (jumpers, thermals) rather than one bulky coat
- Sturdy waterproof shoes or boots
- It rarely gets hot, so a light summer wardrobe is enough for warm spells

Charity shops and student clothing swaps are good cheap sources for coats and jumpers.

*Check your specific city's typical climate before packing — the Met Office (metoffice.gov.uk) publishes UK regional averages and forecasts.*
